Speak HAZWOPER.

PEL, IDLH, LEL, Level B, CRZ. A plain-language glossary of the terms you'll hear on every hazardous waste site.

Learn them all: 40-hourSearch the glossary
ABCDEFHILMNOPRSTU
Action level
A measured concentration, set in the site safety plan or a standard, that triggers more protection, monitoring or a stop-work.
Air-purifying respirator (APR)
A respirator that removes contaminants from the air through filters or cartridges. Used in Level C. It can't supply oxygen.
Awareness level
The first emergency responder level under 1910.120(q)(6)(i): recognize a release, isolate the area, notify, and take no further action.
Buddy system
Organizing workers in pairs or groups so each can watch the other and get help in an emergency. Required in hazardous areas.
CERCLA
The Comprehensive Environmental Response, Compensation, and Liability Act of 1980, known as Superfund. Governs cleanup of uncontrolled hazardous waste sites.
Confined space
A space large enough to enter, with limited entry or exit, not designed for continuous occupancy. Permit-required spaces have serious hazards (29 CFR 1910.146).
Contamination reduction zone
The warm zone between the exclusion zone and the support zone, where decontamination takes place.
Decontamination
Removing or neutralizing contaminants from people and equipment so they don't spread beyond the work area.
Direct-reading instrument
A monitor that gives readings on the spot, such as a multi-gas meter or photoionization detector.
Emergency response
A response by employees from inside or outside the work area to an occurrence that results, or is likely to result, in an uncontrolled release of a hazardous substance.
Engineering controls
Physical changes that reduce exposure, such as ventilation, enclosures or wetting dust. Preferred over PPE where feasible.
Exclusion zone
The hot zone, where contamination is or could be present. Entry requires the specified PPE.
Field experience
Supervised days on site that follow initial training: three days for 40-hour workers and one day for 24-hour workers.
Flash point
The lowest temperature at which a liquid gives off enough vapor to ignite. Lower flash point means a greater fire hazard.
HAZWOPER
Hazardous Waste Operations and Emergency Response, the OSHA standard at 29 CFR 1910.120 (and 1926.65 for construction).
Hazardous substance
A substance that can harm people or the environment, as defined in HAZWOPER by reference to CERCLA, DOT and other lists.
Hazardous waste
A waste that's listed by EPA or shows a hazardous characteristic (ignitable, corrosive, reactive or toxic) under RCRA.
IDLH
Immediately Dangerous to Life or Health: an atmosphere that poses an immediate threat to life, could cause irreversible health effects, or would prevent escape.
Incident command system (ICS)
A standard structure for managing emergencies, with one person or a unified group in command. Required for HAZWOPER emergency response.
Incidental release
A small release that people in the area can safely control at the time, with no significant hazard. Not an emergency response under HAZWOPER.
LEL / LFL
Lower explosive (flammable) limit: the lowest concentration of a gas or vapor in air that can ignite. Monitors often alarm at 10% of the LEL.
Level A
Highest protection: fully encapsulating vapor-tight suit with SCBA or supplied air.
Level B
Highest respiratory protection (SCBA or supplied air) with chemical splash protection for the skin.
Level C
Air-purifying respirator with chemical-resistant clothing. Only when contaminants are known, measured and the respirator is suitable.
Level D
Work uniform with boots, gloves and safety glasses. No respiratory hazard.
Medical surveillance
Medical exams before assignment, periodically and at termination for covered HAZWOPER workers (1910.120(f)).
NIOSH
The National Institute for Occupational Safety and Health, which researches workplace hazards, certifies respirators and publishes recommended exposure limits.
Oxygen-deficient atmosphere
An atmosphere with less than 19.5% oxygen by volume.
PEL
Permissible exposure limit: OSHA's legal limit for exposure to a substance, usually as an 8-hour time-weighted average.
Photoionization detector (PID)
A direct-reading instrument that measures total volatile organic compounds. It can't tell which chemical it's detecting.
PPE
Personal protective equipment: respirators, suits, gloves, boots, eye and face protection and similar gear.
RCRA
The Resource Conservation and Recovery Act, which governs hazardous waste from generation to disposal.
Refresher training
Annual training that keeps HAZWOPER workers current: 8 hours a year for site and TSDF workers.
REL
Recommended exposure limit, set by NIOSH. Often lower than OSHA's PEL.
Respirator fit test
A test that checks a tight-fitting respirator seals to the wearer's face. Required before use and annually.
SCBA
Self-contained breathing apparatus: a respirator with its own air supply, used in Levels A and B.
Safety data sheet (SDS)
The manufacturer's document describing a chemical's hazards, handling, PPE and emergency measures, in 16 sections.
Site characterization
Evaluating a site's hazards before and during work, including a preliminary evaluation before entry (1910.120(c)).
Site safety and health plan
The site-specific plan required by 1910.120(b)(4), covering hazards, PPE, monitoring, site control, decon and emergencies.
STEL
Short-term exposure limit: a 15-minute time-weighted average limit.
Support zone
The cold zone: the clean area where command, supplies and break areas are located.
TLV
Threshold limit value, an exposure guideline published by ACGIH.
TSDF
Treatment, storage and disposal facility: a permitted hazardous waste facility, covered by HAZWOPER paragraph (p).
TWA
Time-weighted average: an exposure averaged over a period, usually 8 hours.
UEL / UFL
Upper explosive (flammable) limit: the highest concentration that can ignite. Above it the mixture is too rich to burn, but still dangerous.
Universal waste
Batteries, pesticides, mercury-containing equipment, lamps and aerosol cans that can be managed under simpler EPA rules (40 CFR 273).

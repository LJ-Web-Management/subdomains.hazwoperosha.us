PPE levels A, B, C and D on one page

The fastest way to remember what each level of protection includes and when it's used.

The four levels of protection come from EPA and are described in Appendix B of OSHA's HAZWOPER standard, 29 CFR 1910.120. The idea is simple: respiratory protection and skin protection are chosen separately, and the level names the combination.

The cheat sheet

LevelBreathingSkinUse when
ASCBA or supplied airFully encapsulating, vapor-tight chemical suitHighest skin and respiratory hazard, or unknown conditions
BSCBA or supplied airChemical-resistant splash suit, not vapor-tightHighest respiratory hazard, lower skin hazard
CAir-purifying respirator (APR or PAPR)Chemical-resistant clothingContaminant known, concentration measured, APR criteria met
DNoneWork uniform, boots, gloves, safety glassesNo respiratory hazard, minimal skin hazard

Memory trick

  • A = All covered, airtight
  • B = Breathing at maximum, splash on skin
  • C = Cartridge respirator
  • D = Daily work clothes

The Level C rule

Level C is only allowed when you know what's in the air, you've measured how much, and an air-purifying respirator is rated for it. That also means oxygen is adequate (at least 19.5%) and conditions aren't immediately dangerous to life or health. If you don't know what's there, you're not in Level C.

Higher isn't always safer

Level A is hot, heavy, limits your vision and wears you out fast. Heat stress is a real risk in A and B. Sites upgrade when monitoring shows they need to, and downgrade when it's safe, so the crew can work longer and with fewer mistakes.

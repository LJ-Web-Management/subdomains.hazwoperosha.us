Vapor intrusion and new buildings on brownfields

Contaminant vapors can move from soil into buildings. How redevelopment projects design for it.

Volatile contaminants in soil and groundwater, such as petroleum and chlorinated solvents, can move up as vapor and into buildings. That's vapor intrusion, and it's one of the main long-term issues on redeveloped brownfields.

How projects address it

  • Vapor barriers: membranes installed under the slab
  • Sub-slab depressurization: piping and fans that vent vapor from beneath the building
  • Passive venting layers that can be made active later
  • Indoor air and sub-slab monitoring after construction

For construction crews

Installing these systems usually happens while the crew is working in or above contaminated soil. That means:

  • Monitoring in excavations and utility trenches, where vapors collect. See OSHA Air Monitoring and Gas Detection Training.
  • PPE and action levels from the site health and safety plan
  • Care not to puncture or damage membranes, which can compromise the system

For site leads

Membranes, seams and penetrations get inspected before the slab is poured. Coordinate inspections, and document everything for the regulator. Site leads directing this work need supervisor training (OSHA 8 Hour HAZWOPER Supervisor Training).
